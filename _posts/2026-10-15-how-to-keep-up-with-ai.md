Software development has always been evolving, as long as I can remember. It constantly evolves, and it follows trends. These trends are usually for the better, and sometimes they don't survive the test of time and they get left behind.

Currently, it's all about artificial intelligence (AI), agentic development, and everything related. But, how should we keep our skills in demand? What does that mean for someone just starting his career? How should people who has been in this field for so long move forward?

The following article is my personal take in that matter. I'll share my honest feeling and how I think we should move forward.

<!--more-->

# Why am I writing this

## Personal experience and feeling

I have recently shared an [AI migration story](https://tiger4589.github.io/2026/10/10/ai-migration-story.html), how it went, what went wrong, and how to leverage the use of AI in your projects.

One specific feeling stuck with me that I also shared in that article is: **The code isn't _mine_ anymore**. It felt that way because since I started coding, almost 20 years ago, I fell in love with it. The feeling of writing something that solved a problem, had some kind of impact on myself or those around me, and later on professionally, gave me a huge satisfaction.

That satisfaction using AI isn't _full_ anymore. Yes, the end result is still the same. The output is a full application that you can probably ship to production after verification and validation. However, the process is no longer the same.

Putting feeling aside, we still have a responsibility to deliver that software. It's our job at the end of the day. As software engineers, we have the bonus of having good analytical skills, and we do adapt easily to changes. Adaptation varies from understanding new businesses during our career when we switch projects, or new requirements in the same project, and managing our pet projects on the side.

## The past few years

I've seen a lot of discussions and posts about what AI can do, and how it will replace software developers. I've also seen people skeptical about the whole thing, and are pretty sure that the bubble will burst soon. However, given the pace that AI is evolving in, and the level it is at the moment, I believe the truth lives between these two groups of people:

**AI isn't going anywhere, and software developers will have to adapt.**

Yes, one person can now migrate small to huge applications alone, from one language to another, from a legacy code to a modern one, but understanding what is being delivered is still critical. Developers must verify behavior through tests, review security implications, understand operational risks, and check whether the solution meets the actual requirements or not. The future is never certain, but I don't see AI totally replacing software developers.

# How to stay competitive in this field?

The following is completely my personal opinion, and how I see things moving forward. I might be completely correct, or completely wrong, or in between, and time will tell.

The way to avoid getting left behind isn't to chase every new tool. It's to develop the judgment to understand what these tools can do, where they fail, and how to use them without losing the skills that made us good engineers.

## Students and new graduates

If you're currently studying, or recently got your degree, don't be afraid.

As much as AI is good at delivering code, it's even more capable of explaining it. Writing code has never been quicker, but it should be understood.

Come up with your own ideas, projects, or tools to build, and do that manually first, without delegating it to AI tools. When stuck or in doubt, don't let AI generate the full solution, but let it guide you to it. You still need to understand the fundamentals of software development, the patterns, the good and the bad. Without these, you won't be able to take the best decisions.

Landing a job won't just be answering the question "do you know how to use AI tool", it will be closer to "How would you manage an AI tool to deliver X, Y, and Z". Understanding the basics is what allows you to answer that.

It's also important to study how to use those tools, don't get me wrong, but focus on answering the "why" questions, and you'll get there.

**Note that even though AI can generate explanations, it might still be confidently wrong. Use AI to accelerate your learning, then verify what you've learned through documentations, experimentation, tests, and independent reasoning.**

## Juniors in the field

Congratulations, you're already building the needed experience. However, you need to **keep learning!** Don't just rely on AI output. There's much more than the basics to learn. Start exploring more design patterns, more system design, and different architectures.

This is just the beginning of a long road.

Ask as many questions as you need to more experienced developers in your team. You need to start understanding more in depth why certain decisions are taken. For example: [why did we use a message broker, or even, why didn't we use a message broker?](https://tiger4589.github.io/2026/09/24/when-to-use-a-message-broker.html)

These continuous questions and learning will slowly pave the path to become a more senior developer burdened with more knowledge.

### Avoid this pitfall

If, as a developer, you keep on delegating tasks that you don't understand to the AI, you might become less capable of detecting incorrect output, debugging the behavior, or making good decisions when the tools fail.

The important balance is not to avoid AI-generated solutions. It's to make sure that as developer, you can explain, test, and take responsibility for the solution.

## Mid-Level Developers

You should already be able to deliver features independently, troubleshoot problems, and write maintainable code.

If your team is dependent on AI, it's time to start taking more ownership of significant technical work. Given the experience you already built up, it should be easier to work alongside these tools, and take the right decisions. Don't get complacent though, this doesn't mean that you should only rely on AI tools from now on. Learning never ends. Gather more experience, specially in architecture and system design. Here's a fun experience that you can do: take an existing service in your codebase, map the dependencies, and ask a senior engineer what's the decision behind the current architecture. Then use AI to explore alternatives and compare those suggestions against your actual requirements.

And regardless if you're currently working with AI or not, it's the perfect time to start handling more complex problems, try to take architectural decisions alongside your senior colleagues, and learn from those decisions.

When AI can generate multiple possible implementations in minutes, the ability to recognize which one will remain maintainable for years to come becomes much more valuable.

## Seniors and more

Given the way things are going, it's time to use all the experience you have to build software faster than ever! AI tools today have proven their worth and what you can do with them, and knowing how the software industry move, this is probably going to be on each job description.

Don't be afraid to adapt. AI make executing ideas and building software much easier now, but they still need experienced developers to maintain, and validate. They need experience to design, and to take the right decisions where it matters.

If you haven't used these tools yet, go ahead, don't fall behind. Trust, but verify!

### How do you preserve your engineering skills?

Professionally: you can review generated code while waiting for some new sessions to end, discuss it with colleagues, and examine the architecture. If met with a bug, use your debugging skills before immediately delegating the problem to another agent. Write some tests that are off-limit for the AI and that they must be green. Being able to explain the system you've built with the AI is a good recipe.

Personally: keep some pet projects around you, toying with them is fun. I love to write code, and while working on fun projects for myself, I still prefer to write as much as I can manually. Once I am happy with the initial architecture and design, I might delegate the work to the AI, instructing it to follow in my footsteps. This keeps me trained on reading the code and being able to follow it through.

## For everyone

Learning doesn't stop. I always give an example that we learn how to use our hands on a daily basis. Coding is a practice, just like anything else in the world.

I personally like to read blogs, technical documentations, and watch some videos if I want a quicker summary. I wouldn't advise to ditch this and only use AI to learn. Remember AI has cut-off dates, and although it can visit the web to give you a summary, it might skip some important notes that can be useful. I've had that happen to me before.

Keep your sources close to you, and keep on learning from them. It will always be handy, no matter what.

My personal learning loop:

- I discover what's new and useful by following trusted sources instead of every announcement.
- I experiment by choosing one development workflow or tool that helps me with a problem. I try it on a small task.
- I evaluate by comparing the result to my normal approach, consider the quality, maintainability, and the effort needed.
- I consolidate by recoding what worked, what failed, and what I've learned.

### How to decide what is worth learning?

Learning something new is always nice and fun, but ask yourself: does it solve a real problem you have? This is coming from experience. Whenever I learn something, without actually applying it anywhere, I tend to forget it. Search for what you need at the moment, and tackle it. Another question worth asking: does it matter with the current hype cycle? If there's a change in your favorite language that is simply a synthetic sugar change, then maybe it's not that worth diving deep into it, but if there's a big update, that might contain some breaking changes, or a whole new way to manage memory, then it's probably worth checking it out.

# What does experience mean?

When AI does most of the implementations, what does experience mean? It isn't simply the ability to write code. It includes recognizing bad abstractions, choosing the appropriate trade-offs, understanding the consequences of decisions.

Applying KISS (Keep it simple, stupid) and YAGNI (You aren't gonna need it) principles is still important. If your AI agent is building abstractions on top of abstractions that you know you wouldn't need, it's better to stop it. It will save you some tokens down the line, and it will allow you to easier understand the system you're building.

If the AI agent decides to use a message broker where a simple HTTP call is sufficient, you have to step in. Knowing when to use what is very important, and choosing the right trade-off has a lot of benefit on how your system will behave once you ship it.

If you need to choose between SignalR or SSE for real-time communication, then you need to understand the consequences of your choice: what are you gaining? What are you losing? What should you be aware of?

This is why continuous learning is important, it will allow you to build the experience you need to be able to take those decisions and understand their trade-offs.

# How to adapt without losing the fun of programming?

I know that not all developers need to enjoy the same thing, or the same workflow. My advice is to do what you enjoy, and delegate the rest. Some might enjoy system designs, and others might still value writing code. Some might enjoy seeing the end product of an idea they have, and others might still prefer to build it slowly.

This might not apply in your job, where you have to adapt as an organization, but it can be used on a personal level to still gain that satisfaction that might be lost.

I personally don't like to get involved too much in front-end development, and I find it much more fulfilling to work on the back-end. I aim to do that on my personal projects as much as possible, and delegate the front-end stuff to AI tools.

# Final thoughts

Does AI mean giving up the satisfaction of creating something? Or does it mean finding satisfaction in different things? Like understanding a complex system, or delivering something that would have previously taken months?

A mentor of mine wrote an article seven years ago. [The day when the software industry makes up its mind](https://adamtibi.com/archives/the-day-when-the-software-industry-makes-its-mind) and it still stands today. The only difference is that the pace of change is much quicker these days, and adapting is becoming heavier than ever, but it is necessary. Don't avoid what's inevitable, but treat it carefully and with open mind. Remember, by the end of the day, you're still responsible for what you're delivering, whether an AI wrote it or yourself.
